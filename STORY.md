# Beyond the Coherence Envelope

The last message from Imperial Command arrived while the stars still had names.

The *Horizon Seeker* hung beside a fracture too small to see with the naked eye. On the bridge, the navigator watched its outline form on the display: a seam in empty space, detected first by the Directorate's quantum-topological surveys and then measured, checked, and measured again. The Imperial Astroscience Directorate called what lay beyond it the Void Continuum. They had found traces of matter there that should not have existed, and curves in its geometry that might allow a ship to cross interstellar distances without crossing the space between them.

The briefing had four objectives. Map the dimension. Catalogue its exotic matter. Determine whether it could be mined, and whether its folds could carry a transit network. Return with data and samples. The navigator was a reconnaissance specialist, not a miner or a passenger on the network the Imperium hoped to build. The *Horizon Seeker* was a prototype, and no vessel had yet gone far enough into the Void to know what a safe distance meant.

The Dimensional Phase Translocator, DPT-MKIV, gave the mission its margin of safety. It held an entangled reference to the point of departure in ordinary space. As long as the ship remained within the system's million-kilometer coherence envelope, the translocator could use that reference to bring it back. The ship's Phase-Locked Hull Lattice held its molecular bonds steady when the laws around them changed. Its field nodes carried a stored template of the hull's original structure; repair filaments could rebuild bonds the field could no longer hold. The navigator watched the tether reading beside the mission clock. Both held steady through the crossing.

On the other side, the first survey was everything the Directorate had hoped for. Crystals registered masses and energy densities that the ship's instruments could report but not explain. Some appeared to occupy more room within their surfaces than around them. The navigator sent readings back to Command, marked promising concentrations, and kept to the planned survey path. A few small positional discrepancies went into the error log. The communications link was clear. The tether was stable. There was reason to believe the ship would be home on schedule.

Then a charted formation appeared where it had not been a minute before.

The navigator checked the engines. They had not fired. The inertial record showed no acceleration, and the nearby fragments had not changed their positions relative to the ship. Only the reading to the departure point had changed. It changed again while the navigator watched.

The first small errors had been easy to blame on unfamiliar sensors. Here, the space holding the ship and its neighboring fragments had shifted together. The instruments built for ordinary travel had tracked movement *through* space; they had not noticed the space itself moving. By the time the navigator compared every survey mark against the distant anchor, the tether was failing.

Command's final transmission broke into pieces. The DPT rejected the recall sequence. The navigator tried it twice more, though its threshold reading had already passed the limit. The ship was too far from its point of entry for the tether to bring it back.

For a time, the navigator kept working through the original survey plan. There was no route home on the charts, but there were readings to collect and an anomaly to understand. A pattern emerged among the regions of greatest drift: each carried a loss of local geometry, as if something had taken a bite from the distance between two places.

Something had.

The first creature appeared at the edge of the sensor field and crossed it without a trajectory the computer could plot. Its body did not keep a consistent shape. Around it, survey markers separated and joined; a fragment that had been close enough to image vanished behind a stretch of space that had not existed a moment earlier. The navigator held the ship clear and watched the creature feed. It consumed the fabric of the Void, leaving the surrounding geometry to settle badly behind it.

There were more of them. The drift was not a harmless property of the dimension. It was damage, spreading from the places where its predators fed. One turned toward the *Horizon Seeker* before the ship's weapons had locked on.

The hull alarm came later. At first it reported tolerances that were merely unusual. Then the Phase-Locked Hull Lattice began to lose its hold on the material beneath it. Void energy intruded through every exposed surface; even the ship's best protection could only resist it, never seal it out. The charge reading climbed. Field nodes corrected each distorted bond against their stored template, but the next intrusion bent it again. Repair filaments worked behind them and fell farther behind. At the rate of exposure then measured, the computer projected complete structural collapse within seventy-two hours. The navigator pulled up the repair inventory, then closed it. There was no replacement hull aboard, and the recall system still had no anchor.

The largest creature on the sensors was nearly two hundred meters across. Its feeding distorted the readings for kilometers around it, but at its center the navigator found a concentrated source of the same energy that moved the surrounding space. A living organ, perhaps. A way across the membrane, perhaps. The computer would offer no estimate of its usefulness. The navigator marked the creature's course and brought every functioning weapon to bear.

The fight outlasted every simulation in the mission archive. The navigator fired, turned, and fired again as distances folded around the target. Once, the creature seemed close enough to strike the cockpit. The next instant the weapons had to search for it beyond their expected range. When its body finally broke apart, the ship was left with scorched plating and a shrinking window of time. The core remained intact among the fragments, a dense crystalline mass that seemed to pulse even after the organism around it was gone.

The navigator brought it aboard.

There was no authorized procedure for putting part of a Void predator into an Imperial engine. The navigator traced the DPT's dead tether circuit to its power assembly and built a second route around it, feeding the core's dimensional flux into the translocator and propulsion systems. Every test could have ruined the only shelter left. The first diagnostic produced a momentary phase shift. The next confirmed that the system could do it again without the lost anchor.

The system could move between dimensions under its own power now. It could not find Imperial space. For one breath, the difference hardly mattered.

The navigator initiated egress.

The bridge lights went out. The hull groaned as the ship passed through the dimensional membrane, and then ordinary stars filled the forward glass. The navigator waited for the astrogation system to name one. It named none. The computer tried a second catalog and a third, finally returning a distance estimate that placed every known Imperial territory thousands of light-years away.

The ship had escaped the Void. It had not come home.

The hull alarm changed tone. In ordinary space, new Void energy no longer seeped into the plating. The stored charge began to bleed away, and the lattice's field nodes could hold their corrections long enough for the repair filaments to reknit the damaged bonds. The structural-collapse estimate vanished from the display. The hull would recover, but the charge already inside it slowed every repair. The navigator still had to keep it clear of everything outside.

Without the old anchor, the core could choose the dimension of arrival but not an Imperial address within it. The space displaced during the long drift had not been restored by crossing back. The navigator looked out at a sky no one in the Directorate had mapped and understood what the successful egress had cost: the ship had crossed the membrane, while the distance it had made was still ahead.

The first realspace contact registered as a moving mass beyond the asteroid field. The second came from another direction. They were not the creatures the navigator had hunted in the Void, though the readings bore a disturbing family resemblance. These predators moved through ordinary space and turned toward the *Horizon Seeker* as soon as its active systems came within range. The ship's electromagnetic emissions had announced its arrival.

Behind it, the violent exit had left smaller wounds in spacetime. Sensor returns that first looked like errors resolved into micro-singularities, some already drawing together. Ahead lay debris from an ancient stellar collapse. The asteroid field brought immediate collision risk, but also the mass the ship needed. Its converter could recover matter from shattered rock and synthesize antimatter with power drawn from the creature's core. Confined separately until the reactor needed them, the two halves would become matter-antimatter fuel: M-AM. The conversion was costly, but a small reserve could drive the ship a long way, and there was more rock ahead than the ship could carry. The ship had escaped one dangerous place only to find that its passage had made this one more dangerous.

During the desperate retrofit, the navigator had found a way to focus Void energy through a weapon coil. The resulting laser was a discharge path as well as a gun. Inside the Void, ambient energy intruded as fast as the beam could carry it away; here, each shot would shed some of the ship's stored charge. Turns and bursts of thrust drew from that charge too, making every necessary maneuver part of the discharge. The core could drive the ship faster than its M-AM hyper mode without burning fuel; the helm kept its familiar turning rate. The faster the charge left, the sooner the lattice could restore the hull. The navigator could wait for a slow natural bleed, but the approaching predators gave the ship no time to waste.

The navigator opened the hull-lattice readings from the Void and began a new design. The core had made phase travel possible, but every deliberate crossing would expose the ship to another surge of intrusive energy. If the charge gauge filled, the field nodes would lose their lock long before the repair filaments could help. The proposed Energy Modulator placed phase gates between the core, the hull lattice, and the ship's control circuits. At low charge, thrusting, turning, or firing would still draw useful Void energy in faster; above the safe midpoint, those same actions would reverse the gates and bleed charge away. Left idle inside the Void, the ship would still absorb it. The design remained in the construction queue. It needed Incident Essence from the hostile creatures now closing in before the navigator could build it.

The navigator entered a new course. The Imperium was too distant for a direct flight, and the core's improvised phase drive was too uncertain for a blind jump. The ship would have to travel, gather what it could, and learn what the Void had made possible. Each encounter would add another record to the mission archive. If the navigator reached Imperial space, those records might keep the next ship from crossing the fracture unprepared.

The original briefing remained open on a corner of the display. Its final objective was still there: *Return with data and samples.* The navigator left it where it was and brought the weapons online.

The nearest asteroid filled the forward glass. The navigator turned toward it and thrust on the stored charge; the fuel gauge held steady as the ship surged ahead. A line of violet light split the rock. The navigator kept firing into the debris until the last charge left the hull and the repair filaments could finish their work. The converter took in the fragments, beginning a new reserve of M-AM. Beyond them, something vast shifted course toward the ship.
